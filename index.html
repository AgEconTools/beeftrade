The beef import quota and tariffs

Each year a set amount of beef can enter the United States at a low tariff. Beef past that amount pays 26.4%, unless a trade agreement covers it. Canada and Mexico trade beef duty-free under USMCA and are outside the quota. Figures are fresh, frozen and processed beef, in pounds of product weight, unless noted.

"Beef" on this page means fresh, frozen and processed beef together. Variety meats are counted on their own, chosen with the Product menu, and never added into beef totals. Veal is part of beef in these codes.

How it's measured

  • Imports count beef entered for use in the United States, including beef taken out of bonded warehouses (Census calls these imports for consumption). That is the basis the quota is charged on.
  • Exports are domestic exports only: beef and cattle of U.S. origin. Re-exports of imported beef are left out.
  • Pounds are product weight as shipped, converted from Census's kilograms (1 kg = 2.20462 lb). USDA's supply and use tables convert trade to carcass weight, so their totals run higher than this page's.
  • Dollars for imports are customs value: the price paid, not counting freight and insurance to the United States or duties. For exports, dollars are the value at the U.S. port (free alongside ship). Price per pound is dollars divided by pounds: an average declared value, not a market price. Prices based on under 10,000 lb are marked few lb.
  • Duties are Census's calculated duty: the tariff owed on each shipment by its tariff line and value, including the extra tariffs of 2025. The average tariff is duties divided by customs value.
  • Seasonal pattern: each month's value against the centered 12-month average around it (a 2x12 moving average: 13 months, the two ends at half weight), averaged over the last five full years.

Comparing with other sources

Beef here is defined the same way as USDA ERS's beef and veal (fresh, frozen and processed; variety meats separate). ERS reports carcass weight, so its totals run about 35% higher. USMEF's export figures also count variety meats and tripe, so they run higher for Mexico and Japan.

How beef enters

Each import is grouped by how it entered:

  • Canada and Mexico (USMCA): duty-free, outside the quota.
  • Country quotas: within a country's own allocation under the WTO beef quota (see the quota table), at the low in-quota tariff.
  • Other-countries quota: within the shared quota for all other countries, including the 2026 lean trimmings tranches (Proclamation 11059), at the low in-quota tariff.
  • Over quota: beef entered on the over-quota tariff lines, after a quota filled. The over-quota tariff is 26.4%; in 2025 extra tariffs applied on top. When Australia's own quota fills, its beef shows up on these lines too. Census's calculated duty puts that beef at the 26.4% rate in November and December 2025, although the U.S.-Australia agreement provides duty-free treatment above the WTO quota when it is claimed. Some beef on these lines from other countries was declared duty-free under chapter 99 provisions, mostly in December 2025 and January 2026. Duties here are as Census calculates them from entry records; later corrections or refunds are not captured.
  • Other trade agreements: duty-free under other U.S. trade agreements (in practice, CAFTA-DR countries and Chile), not charged to the quota.
  • Outside the beef quota: processed beef and variety meats, which the quota doesn't cover, plus a little beef on other tariff lines.

The quota

The U.S. beef tariff-rate quota sets yearly amounts, in metric tons of product, for each country with its own allocation and for all other countries together. Other countries together had 65,005 t through 2025, before the United Kingdom's 13,000 t was split off in 2026.

Two 2026 proclamations added room for lean beef trimmings (HTS 0201.30.5091, 0201.30.5097, 0202.30.5091 and 0202.30.5097) at the in-quota tariff. Proclamation 11010, "Ensuring Affordable Beef for the American Consumer" (February 2026), added 80,000 t for Argentina in four quarterly tranches of 20,000 t, the first opening February 13, on top of Argentina's 20,000 t allocation. Proclamation 11059, "Further Ensuring Affordable Beef for the American Consumer" (August 2026), added 300,000 t for other countries in three tranches of 100,000 t: September 1 to 30, October 1 to 30, and October 31 to November 30. It makes no provision for unused room to carry to the next tranche.

Australian beef also has a separate quantity under the U.S.-Australia Free Trade Agreement (set each year by USTR), the level used for the agreement's price-based safeguard on beef. CBP counts entries against it, and the quota table shows it.

U.S. Customs and Border Protection reports how full each quota is every week; this page shows its newest report. CBP's quota counts and Census's monthly trade totals are compiled differently and will not match exactly. 1 metric ton (t) = 2,204.6 lb.

Tariffs in 2025

Over-quota beef pays 26.4%. In 2025 extra tariffs applied on top: a 10% tariff on most countries from April 5, and an extra 40% on Brazil from August 6. Beef was exempted from both effective November 13, 2025 (the Brazil order was signed November 20 and applied from November 13). The temporary 10% import surcharge of February 24 to July 24, 2026 (Proclamation 11012) excluded beef. Duties for December 2025 and January 2026 are low because most over-quota entries were declared duty-free (see the note under the duties table).

Live cattle

Head counts of feeder and slaughter cattle crossing the border with Canada and Mexico. Breeding and dairy cattle are left out. Imports from Mexico were halted at times from late 2024 to keep New World screwworm out of the United States, and those months show few or none.

Imports are split by their tariff lines into feeder cattle (calves and feeders under 705 lb, or 320 kg, plus heavier cattle not entered for slaughter or breeding) and slaughter cattle (steers, heifers, cows and bulls entered for immediate slaughter). USDA's weekly reports of cattle imports from Canada count the same classes, and their yearly totals agree with these within about 3%. Exports can't be split: Census export records put feeder and slaughter cattle under one code, along with breeding stock that isn't purebred. Exports count only Canada and Mexico, the two markets for U.S. feeder and slaughter cattle; the few hundred head a year sent anywhere else are breeding and dairy animals.

When it updates

The U.S. Census Bureau releases each month's trade about five weeks after the month ends, and revises recent months in later releases. U.S. Customs and Border Protection updates its quota reports weekly.
