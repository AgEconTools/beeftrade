    The beef import quota and tariffs

    Each year a set amount of beef can enter the United States at a low tariff. Beef past that amount pays 26.4%, unless a trade agreement covers it. Canada and Mexico trade beef duty-free under USMCA and are outside the quota. Figures are fresh, frozen and processed beef unless noted.

    About the data

    What's counted, how it's measured, and where it comes from.

    "Beef" on this page means fresh, frozen and processed beef together. Variety meats are counted on their own, chosen with the Product menu, and never added into beef totals. Veal is part of beef in these codes.

    How it's measured

    • Imports count beef entered for use in the United States, including beef taken out of bonded warehouses (Census calls these imports for consumption). That is the basis the quota is charged on.
    • Exports are domestic exports only: beef and cattle of U.S. origin. Re-exports of imported beef are left out.
    • Pounds are product weight as shipped, not carcass weight, converted from Census's kilograms (1 kg = 2.20462 lb).
    • Dollars for imports are customs value: the price paid, not counting freight and insurance to the United States or duties. For exports, dollars are the value at the U.S. port (free alongside ship). Price per pound is dollars divided by pounds: an average declared value, not a market price. Prices based on under 10,000 lb are marked few lb.
    • Duties are Census's calculated duty: the tariff Census calculates for each shipment from its tariff line and value, including the extra tariffs of 2025. The average tariff is duties divided by customs value.
    • Seasonal pattern: each month's value against the centered 12-month average around it (a 2x12 moving average: 13 months, the two ends at half weight), averaged over the last five full years, then scaled so the twelve months average zero.

    Comparing with other sources

    Beef here matches USDA ERS's beef and veal. ERS and USDA's supply and use tables report carcass weight, so their totals run about 35% higher than this page's product weight. USMEF's export figures also count variety meats and tripe, so they run higher for Mexico and Japan.

    How beef enters

    Each import is grouped by how it entered:

    • Canada and Mexico (USMCA): duty-free, outside the quota.
    • Country quotas: within a country's own allocation (see the quota table), including Argentina's added 2026 lean trimmings quota, at the low in-quota tariff.
    • Other-countries quota: within the shared quota for all other countries, at the low in-quota tariff. Small amounts can still show in months after it fills, entered at the low in-quota tariff.
    • Lean trimmings quota: lean beef trimmings from countries without their own allocation, entered in quota while the 2026 tranches were open (Proclamation 11059, September to November 2026), at the low in-quota tariff. Argentina's lean trimmings quota is counted under Country quotas.
    • Over quota: beef entered on the over-quota tariff lines after a quota filled, normally at 26.4%. Australian beef entered after its own quota fills is grouped here too, though the U.S.-Australia agreement lets it enter duty-free. See Reading the duty figures on the Quota tab.
    • Other trade agreements: duty-free under other U.S. trade agreements (in practice, CAFTA-DR countries and Chile), not charged to the quota.
    • Outside the beef quota: processed beef, which the quota doesn't cover. Variety meats fall here only when chosen in the Product menu.

    The quota

    The U.S. beef tariff-rate quota sets yearly amounts, in metric tons of product, for each country with its own allocation and for all other countries together. Other countries together had 65,005 t through 2025, before the United Kingdom's 13,000 t was split off in 2026.

    Two 2026 proclamations added room for lean beef trimmings (HTS 0201.30.5091, 0201.30.5097, 0202.30.5091 and 0202.30.5097) at the in-quota tariff. Proclamation 11010, "Ensuring Affordable Beef for the American Consumer" (February 2026), added 80,000 t for Argentina in four quarterly tranches of 20,000 t, the first opening February 13, on top of Argentina's 20,000 t allocation. Proclamation 11059, "Further Ensuring Affordable Beef for the American Consumer" (August 2026), added 300,000 t for other countries in three tranches of 100,000 t: September 1 to 30, October 1 to 30, and October 31 to November 30. It makes no provision for unused room to carry to the next tranche.

    Australian beef also has a separate quantity under the U.S.-Australia Free Trade Agreement (set each year by USTR), the level used for the agreement's price-based safeguard on beef. The note under the quota table explains it.

    CBP's quota counts and Census's monthly trade totals are compiled differently and will not match exactly. 1 metric ton (t) = 2,204.6 lb.

    Tariffs in 2025

    Over-quota beef pays 26.4%. In 2025 extra tariffs applied to beef from most countries, whether it entered in quota or over quota: a 10% tariff from April 5, raised from August 7 for some suppliers, including New Zealand and Costa Rica (15%) and Nicaragua (18%), and an extra 40% on Brazil from August 6. Beef from Canada and Mexico that qualified under USMCA was exempt. Beef was exempted from both effective November 13, 2025 (the Brazil order was signed November 20 and applied from November 13). The temporary 10% import surcharge of February 24 to July 24, 2026 (Proclamation 11012) excluded beef.

    How Census's records classify over-quota beef (dutiable, or free under a heading in chapter 99 of the U.S. tariff schedule, the chapter for temporary tariff changes), as described in Reading the duty figures on the Quota tab, comes from the U.S. International Trade Commission's DataWeb, which breaks imports out by the tariff provision they were declared under.

    Live cattle

    Head counts of feeder and slaughter cattle crossing the border with Canada and Mexico. Breeding and dairy cattle are left out. Imports from Mexico were halted at times from late 2024 to keep New World screwworm out of the United States, and those months show few or none. Imports from Mexico resumed in phases beginning August 24, 2026.

    Imports are split by their tariff lines into feeder cattle (calves and feeders under 705 lb, or 320 kg, plus heavier cattle not entered for slaughter or breeding) and slaughter cattle (steers, heifers, cows and bulls entered for immediate slaughter). USDA's weekly reports of cattle imports from Canada count the same classes, and their yearly totals agree with these within about 3%. Exports can't be split: Census export records put feeder and slaughter cattle under one code, along with breeding stock that isn't purebred. Exports count only Canada and Mexico, the two markets for U.S. feeder and slaughter cattle; the few hundred head a year sent anywhere else are breeding and dairy animals.

    When it updates

    The U.S. Census Bureau releases each month's trade about five weeks after the month ends, and revises recent months in later releases. U.S. Customs and Border Protection updates its quota reports weekly; this page shows the newest.
